01

Find a beginning that explains the attempt

Look for the earliest moment a new viewer needs in order to understand the shot. A basketball clip may need the pass, player movement or a brief view of the shooter preparing. Starting precisely at release can remove that explanation. Use the recorded action, rather than a fixed number of seconds, to choose the opening.

02

Leave space for the visible result

A basketball clip ending at the top of the arc leaves the play unresolved. In this example, the selections include flight and finish. When trimming your own recording, inspect what happens after the ball reaches the basket before choosing an endpoint. Keep enough picture to make the result understandable, including when it differs from your expectation.

03

Compare a tight cut with a wider cut

Mark a first choice, then try a little more source context on each side. Watch both as a basketball clip instead of deciding only from a still frame. The wider option may explain a setup better; the tight option may remove empty time. Keep the version that communicates the play most clearly at ordinary speed.

04

Record real source boundaries

The video on this page is an already edited sequence. Its playback clock is not the timestamp of the original recording. In your own notes, enter the source filename and boundaries only after locating them in the original file. Do not copy the seven-second runtime into a source log or invent separate cut points for these shots.

Questions about this basketball clip

Where should a clip begin?

Include enough recorded setup for a viewer to understand the attempt; the right boundary depends on what happens before release.

When should it end?

Keep the visible flight and result when those details matter to the play. Review the source rather than relying on a fixed duration.
